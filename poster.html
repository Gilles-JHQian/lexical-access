The poster story
01What is Lexical Access?

When we hear speech, the brain searches for a match in our mental lexicon—the words we know—so that we can recognize what was said.

This process, called lexical access, is a gateway to understanding spoken language. Studying it helps explain how a stream of sound becomes something meaningful.

Comparing words and pseudowords

A common approach is to compare neural responses to familiar words and pronounceable pseudowords: speech sounds that could be words, but have no familiar entry in the mental lexicon. Listen to two examples from our experiment.

A familiar word with a meaning.

Both begin with /æl/, as in the start of “altitude.” Pseudoword spelling follows the dataset’s phonetic transcription; these are the original stimulus recordings.

What previous studies tell us

Word–pseudoword comparisons have mapped a distributed network: pseudowords often elicit stronger responses in superior temporal regions, while words recruit additional temporal and inferior prefrontal regions.1 Lexical responses have also been studied without an overt behavioral response, including in patients with disorders of consciousness.2

When do these networks respond?

fMRI identifies where responses occur, but its slow blood-oxygen signal makes it difficult to separate the rapid stages of word recognition.4 We use intracranial recordings to follow the finer timing of activation across networks.

What is lexical, and what depends on the task?

Word–pseudoword differences can also reflect attention, decision-making and response demands. Which processes are shared when we simply listen, make a decision, or speak?

02Our Design

The same spoken stimuli, with three different response demands.

Twenty-two participants heard 80 stimuli—40 words and 40 pseudowords—during passive listening, lexical decision and overt repetition.

Passive + active tasks

Passive listening requires no output. Processes shared with decision and repetition help identify the perceptual component of lexical access that is present without a required response.

Decision + repetition

A button press and a spoken response have different output demands. Their shared processes help identify lexical processing that links perception to a behavioral goal across output modalities.

Intracranial EEG lets us follow high-gamma activity (HGA) through time. We first identify task-related activation, then compare words and pseudowords within those networks. Finally, we test whether lexicality information transfers between tasks.

Recording and analysis

The poster reports 22 participants (10 female; mean age 32.4 years), 2,555 bipolar sEEG electrode pairs, and 1,596 pairs in cortical regions defined by the Destrieux atlas.3

HGA (70–200 Hz) was extracted around stimulus onset, offset and response onset and z-scored against the 0.5-second pre-cue baseline. The interactive views below provide the available stimulus-onset and stimulus-offset summaries.

Task comparisons help distinguish shared and task-dependent processes; shared activity alone does not isolate a purely lexical mechanism from attention or other shared processing.

03Shared Auditory Activation and Task-Dependent Recruitment

Significant HGA increases relative to baseline reveal auditory temporal recruitment across all three tasks. Active tasks recruit additional frontotemporal responses later in time.

Where activity rises is the first step. Next, ask where that activity differs between words and pseudowords. Explore HGA contrast ↓

04Shared and Task-Dependent Lexicality Effects

Within the task-activated networks, we compare HGA between words and pseudowords and identify significant lexicality effects in either direction.

These contrasts suggest two levels of lexical processing: one present without a required response, and another recruited by response demands but shared across output modalities.

05Shared Lexical Representations Across Tasks

Does a word–pseudoword distinction learned in one task carry over to another?

To test for shared lexical representations, we trained a linear classifier on HGA to distinguish words from pseudowords in one task, then tested it on the other tasks.

06Take home message
Lexical processing includes a component present without behavioral output and additional components recruited by output demands yet shared across output modalities.

When does each network unfold?

Next, we will quantify activation onset, peak and duration relative to stimulus onset and offset.

What explains the activity?

Encoding models will test which acoustic, phonological and lexical–semantic features explain activity in each network.
